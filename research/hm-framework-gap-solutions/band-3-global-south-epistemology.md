## 1. Global South Fact-Checking Methodology: Structural Differences from Western Models

### 1.1 Africa Check (South Africa, est. 2012)

Africa Check, headquartered in Johannesburg, uses an **eight-point methodology** to flag, investigate, and report suspicious claims. Both an IFCN signatory and the founder of the **Africa Facts Network** (20+ member organizations across the continent), Africa Check exemplifies a coalition model that differs structurally from Western fact-checkers.

**Key structural differences from Western fact-checkers:**

- **Coalition-first architecture**: Rather than scaling a single newsroom, Africa Check "incubates" emerging fact-checking organizations across the continent, providing operational and financial support. This contrasts with Western models (e.g., Snopes, PolitiFact) that scale centrally. [WEB-SOURCE: [Reuters Institute - The Fight for Facts in the Global South](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/fight-facts-global-south-how-four-projects-are-building-new-model)]

- **Offline-first distribution**: In Sub-Saharan Africa, where internet access is uneven and mobile data expensive, Africa Check deploys **radio dramas**, community radio partnerships, and WhatsApp helplines to reach offline audiences. Western fact-checkers rarely design for offline-first contexts. [WEB-SOURCE: [Reuters Institute](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/fight-facts-global-south-how-four-projects-are-building-new-model)]

- **Multilingual reach**: Content produced in **Isizulu, Swahili, Hausa, and Wolof** alongside English, acknowledging that a single-language approach systematically excludes communities whose primary knowledge-sharing happens in indigenous languages. [WEB-SOURCE: [Reuters Institute](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/fight-facts-global-south-how-four-projects-are-building-new-model)]

- **Financial independence model**: Africa Check established **TRi Facts**, a separate consulting entity that provides corporate training to fund journalism operations, avoiding both advertising revenue and government funding dependencies. [WEB-SOURCE: [Reuters Institute](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/fight-facts-global-south-how-four-projects-are-building-new-model)]

- **Context-dependent verification**: Africa Check must contend with "unreliable or incredible data or a lack of freedom of information laws" (Laura Zommer, Chequeado), requiring verification methods that don't rely on transparent government databases or accessible institutional data -- a luxury Western fact-checkers often take for granted. [WEB-SOURCE: [Reuters Institute](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/fight-facts-global-south-how-four-projects-are-building-new-model)]

- **Infodemic response infrastructure**: During COVID-19, Africa Check participated in the **Africa Infodemic Response Alliance (AIRA)** with WHO Africa and **Viral Facts Africa**, a social content hub that produces and disseminates counter-misinformation content in close collaboration with public health institutions and local fact-checkers. [WEB-SOURCE: [WHO AFRO - AIRA](https://www.afro.who.int/aira)]

**H&M implication**: The pipeline's evidence-gathering agents currently privilege digitally accessible, English-language institutional sources. Africa Check's model suggests the pipeline needs (a) multi-language source integration, (b) recognition that authoritative data may not exist in institutional form, and (c) coalition-sourced verification rather than single-source authority.

### 1.2 Chequeado (Argentina, est. 2010)

Chequeado is the **first Latin American fact-checking outlet** and leads **LatamChequea**, a network of 48 organizations across 21 Spanish/Portuguese-speaking countries. [WEB-SOURCE: [LatamChequea - About Us](https://latamchequea.com/en/about-us/)]

**Distinctive methodological features:**

- **Broader scope of accountability**: Chequeado fact-checks not only politicians but also business leaders and media figures, reflecting a Latin American context where corporate and media power are entangled with political power in ways Western fact-checkers often bracket. [WEB-SOURCE: [Reuters Institute](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/fight-facts-global-south-how-four-projects-are-building-new-model)]

- **Community-participatory verification ("Chequeaton")**: Chequeado runs community events where college students and community members perform fact-checking themselves, building verification as a civic skill rather than a journalist-only practice. [WEB-SOURCE: [LatAm Journalism Review](https://latamjournalismreview.org/articles/network-of-fact-checking-trainers-created-to-bridge-the-training-gap-in-latin-american-universities/)]

- **AI-assisted but community-driven**: **Chequeabot** (launched 2018) scans speeches and parliamentary proceedings, generating approximately 50% of initial fact-checks, but community input drives prioritization and final verification. **Aos Fatos** (Brazil) developed **Fatima** (Twitter/Facebook/WhatsApp monitor) and **Radar** (misinformation spread tracker). [WEB-SOURCE: [Reuters Institute](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/fight-facts-global-south-how-four-projects-are-building-new-model)]

- **Trainer network model**: Rather than positioning itself as the authority, Chequeado builds "a community of trainers who can keep in touch through different channels and share resources, strategies and experiences." [WEB-SOURCE: [LatAm Journalism Review](https://latamjournalismreview.org/articles/network-of-fact-checking-trainers-created-to-bridge-the-training-gap-in-latin-american-universities/)]

**H&M implication**: The pipeline could adopt a "participatory verification" layer where community members from affected regions contribute context, rather than relying solely on institutional expert voices. The Chequeabot/human hybrid model is directly analogous to what H&M could implement.

### 1.3 BOOM (India, est. 2014)

BOOM is India's first IFCN-certified fact-checker, operating in **English, Hindi, and Bengali** with dedicated on-ground teams and expansion to Bangladesh and Myanmar. [WEB-SOURCE: [BOOM - About Us](https://www.boomlive.in/about-us)]

**Seven-step methodology** [WEB-SOURCE: [BOOM Methodology](https://www.boomlive.in/methodology)]:
1. Claim selection (community-driven via WhatsApp tipline)
2. Source tracing (reverse image search, origin investigation)
3. Source contact (reaching out to relevant parties)
4. Evidence gathering (government databases, think tanks, research)
5. Expert consultation (subject specialists on record)
6. Story writing (with full citation transparency)
7. Error correction (visible correction advisories)

**Distinctive features:**

- **WhatsApp tipline (+91 7700 906 588)**: Community members submit viral claims directly, making the verification agenda community-driven rather than newsroom-driven. This is critical in India where misinformation spreads primarily through encrypted messaging. [WEB-SOURCE: [BOOM Methodology](https://www.boomlive.in/methodology)]

- **Rural penetration through NGO partnerships**: BOOM reaches rural villages through partnerships with ground-level NGOs, recognizing that digital-only distribution systematically excludes India's rural majority. [WEB-SOURCE: [Reuters Institute](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/fight-facts-global-south-how-four-projects-are-building-new-model)]

- **Regional language teams with local knowledge**: Dedicated Hindi and Bengali teams bring region-specific cultural knowledge to verification, not just translation. [WEB-SOURCE: [BOOM - About Us](https://www.boomlive.in/about-us)]

### 1.4 Maldita.es (Spain)

While not Global South per se, Maldita.es pioneered a **community "superpowers" model** directly relevant to H&M's structural needs.

- **Tiered volunteer system**: General Malditos (newsletter), Ambassadors (financial supporters), and active **"superpowers" contributors** who bring verified subject-area expertise to fact-checking. The expertise form allows members to upload proof of credentials (LinkedIn, diplomas), tagged categorically by community managers. [WEB-SOURCE: [Membership Guide - How Maldita Enlists Members' Superpowers](https://membershipguide.org/case-study/how-maldita-enlists-member-superpowers-to-fact-check/)]

- **Three-filter bias mitigation**: Every verification passes through three independent internal reviews to mitigate individual journalist bias, then classified on a four-level veracity traffic light. [WEB-SOURCE: [EU DisinfoLab](https://www.disinfo.eu/face/journalism-not-to-be-fooled/)]

- **Community-driven agenda**: The team primarily debunks stories brought by its volunteer community, not stories selected by editors. Virality (community reports + social media activity) and dangerousness (crisis situations, public health, social coexistence) determine what gets checked. [WEB-SOURCE: [Membership Guide](https://membershipguide.org/case-study/how-maldita-enlists-member-superpowers-to-fact-check/)]

**H&M implication**: Maldita.es's "superpowers" model provides a concrete template for incorporating domain experts from affected communities into the pipeline's verification process.

### 1.5 Cross-Cutting Research on Fact-Checking Effectiveness

A simultaneous experiment across Argentina, Nigeria, South Africa, and the United Kingdom found that **fact-checks reduced false beliefs in all countries studied**, with most effects detectable more than 2 weeks later and with "surprisingly little variation by country." [PEER-REVIEWED: [PNAS - The Global Effectiveness of Fact-Checking](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.2104235118)]

This is significant for H&M: it suggests that structurally including Global South fact-checking perspectives is not merely an equity concern but also an effectiveness one. The evidence base for fact-checking efficacy spans Global South contexts.

### 1.6 Synthesis: What Global South Fact-Checkers Do Differently

| Dimension | Western Model | Global South Model |
|-----------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Distribution** | Digital-first, web/social media | Offline-first (radio, WhatsApp, community events) |
| **Agenda-setting** | Editor-driven | Community-driven (tiplines, participatory events) |
| **Language** | Predominantly English | Multi-language with regional teams |
| **Authority** | Institutional databases, government data | Hybrid: institutional + community knowledge + NGO networks |
| **Scale model** | Central newsroom scaling | Coalition/network incubation |
| **Scope** | Political claims primarily | Political + corporate + media + community health |
| **Verification** | Expert-to-public | Community-participatory |
| **Context** | Data-rich environments assumed | Data-scarce, FOI-limited environments designed for |

## 2. Decolonial Epistemology and Epistemic Justice in Automated Systems

### 2.1 Santos: Epistemologies of the South (2014)

Boaventura de Sousa Santos's foundational work argues that Western epistemological dominance constitutes **"cognitive injustice"**: the systematic failure to recognize different ways of knowing by which people across the globe run their lives and provide meaning to their existence. [PEER-REVIEWED: Santos, B. de Sousa (2014). *Epistemologies of the South: Justice Against Epistemicide*. Routledge.]

**Core concepts relevant to H&M:**

- **Abyssal lines**: Western epistemology draws invisible boundaries that render Southern knowledges not merely inferior but *non-existent*. Knowledge that doesn't fit Western evidentiary standards is treated as if it doesn't exist at all. [PEER-REVIEWED: Santos 2014]

- **Epistemicide**: The active destruction of knowledge systems and the social practices and agents that sustain them. Colonial and post-colonial power structures don't just suppress alternative knowledge; they destroy the conditions under which such knowledge can be produced and transmitted. [PEER-REVIEWED: Santos 2014]

- **Ecology of knowledges**: Santos proposes replacing the "monoculture of scientific knowledge" with an "ecology of knowledges" where different knowledge systems (scientific, indigenous, experiential) are recognized as valid for different purposes, without collapsing into relativism. [PEER-REVIEWED: Santos 2014]

- **Cognitive justice as prerequisite**: Santos argues that global social justice is impossible without global cognitive justice. You cannot address material inequality while maintaining the epistemic structures that render affected communities' knowledge invisible. [PEER-REVIEWED: Santos 2014]

**H&M implication**: The pipeline's current evidence hierarchy implicitly draws "abyssal lines": peer-reviewed Western research > institutional reports > community testimony. Santos would argue this structure performs epistemicide by design, rendering Global South experiential knowledge non-existent in the analysis. The "ecology of knowledges" framework suggests H&M needs a pluralistic evidence model where different knowledge types are assessed for validity within their own epistemic frameworks, not solely against Western scientific standards.

### 2.2 Ndlovu-Gatsheni: Epistemic Freedom

Sabelo J. Ndlovu-Gatsheni (Professor, University of Bayreuth; Chair of Epistemologies of the Global South with Emphasis on Africa) extends Santos's framework with the concept of **"epistemic freedom"**, which he distinguishes from academic freedom. [WEB-SOURCE: [Routledge - Epistemic Freedom in Africa](https://www.routledge.com/Epistemic-Freedom-in-Africa-Deprovincialization-and-Decolonization/Ndlovu-Gatsheni/p/book/9781138588592)]

**Key arguments:**

- **Intellectual division of labor**: African scholars have historically functioned as "hunter-gatherers" of raw data and "native informants," while Europe and North America process that data into concepts and theories consumed back in Africa. This asymmetry is *structural*, not a matter of individual bias. [PEER-REVIEWED: Ndlovu-Gatsheni, S.J. (2021). "The cognitive empire, politics of knowledge and African intellectual productions." *Third World Quarterly*, 42(5).]

- **Deprovincializing Africa / Provincializing Europe**: Ndlovu-Gatsheni calls for a dual process: centering Africa as a legitimate epistemic site from which to interpret the world, while simultaneously recognizing Europe as one provincial knowledge tradition among many, not the universal standard. [PEER-REVIEWED: Ndlovu-Gatsheni 2018/2021]

- **Epistemic freedom as prerequisite for other freedoms**: Political, cultural, and economic freedoms cannot be achieved until the problem of epistemic freedom is addressed. The over-representation of European thought in knowledge, theory, and education is the root constraint. [PEER-REVIEWED: Ndlovu-Gatsheni 2018]

**H&M implication**: If the pipeline's analytical frameworks (its "theories" about why misinformation spreads, what counts as valid debunking, how to assess equity critiques) are all derived from Western academic traditions, it replicates the "intellectual division of labor" Ndlovu-Gatsheni describes, even if it cites Global South data. The pipeline must not only cite Global South sources but adopt Global South analytical frameworks.

### 2.3 Mbembe: Decolonizing Knowledge and the Archive

Achille Mbembe (Wits University, Johannesburg) contributes two key insights:

- **Decolonization must itself be decolonized**: Mbembe argues that decolonization should be "reconceived in terms that are themselves built on African knowledge rather than a mimesis of (already colonized) North American race relations." He draws on Jane Guyer's description of African knowledge systems as "open, constantly revised and reconfigured" -- suggesting an epistemological tradition that is inherently adaptive, not static. [WEB-SOURCE: [Cambridge - Decolonizing the Virtual](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/african-studies-review/article/decolonizing-the-virtual-future-knowledges-and-the-extrahuman-in-africa/CB945D7E54FB7861F0BA72B98946036F)]

- **Epistemic coloniality**: The dominant Eurocentric academic model involves "the endless production of theories that are based on European traditions" while treating other traditions as raw material or case studies rather than sources of theory. [WEB-SOURCE: [Cambridge - Decolonizing the Virtual](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/african-studies-review/article/decolonizing-the-virtual-future-knowledges-and-the-extrahuman-in-africa/CB945D7E54FB7861F0BA72B98946036F)]

**H&M implication**: The pipeline should not simply add Global South citations to a fundamentally Western analytical framework. That would replicate what Mbembe calls epistemic coloniality. Instead, it needs to engage with non-Western analytical traditions on their own terms, for example using frameworks like Ubuntu philosophy or indigenous epistemologies as lenses for analysis, not just as data points within a Western framework.

### 2.4 Fricker's Epistemic Injustice Applied to AI Systems

Miranda Fricker's framework identifies two forms of epistemic injustice, both of which have been applied to automated systems by recent researchers.

**Testimonial injustice**: When prejudice leads to undermining someone's credibility as a knower -- e.g., when accent, gender, or racial identity causes others to doubt their truthfulness. [PEER-REVIEWED: Fricker, M. (2007). *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing*. Oxford University Press.]

**Hermeneutical injustice**: When marginalized groups lack access to shared interpretive resources needed to make sense of their social experiences. [PEER-REVIEWED: Fricker 2007]

### 2.5 Kay, Kasirzadeh & Mohamed: Epistemic Injustice in Generative AI (2024)

This paper develops a **four-part taxonomy** of how generative AI systems produce epistemic injustice [PREPRINT: Kay, Kasirzadeh & Mohamed (2024). "Epistemic Injustice in Generative AI." arXiv:2408.11441]:

1. **Amplified testimonial injustice**: AI magnifies biased viewpoints from training data; the "credibility excess assigned to the algorithm" outweighs marginalized individuals' lived experiences.

2. **Manipulative testimonial injustice**: Intentional manipulation of AI to fabricate false content targeting marginalized groups (the "liar's dividend" of deepfakes).

3. **Generative hermeneutical ignorance**: AI systems lack cultural and embodied knowledge, misrepresenting marginalized experiences through "willful hermeneutical ignorance."

4. **Hermeneutical access injustice**: Unequal linguistic and informational access based on identity creates knowledge gaps, particularly through multilingual AI disparities.

**Proposed solutions from the paper:**
- Participatory development incorporating affected communities
- Equitable data representation through **data sovereignty**
- Epistemic transparency via improved documentation
- Diverse alignment processes avoiding dominant-group perspectives
- Using generative AI *for* justice: identifying testimonial injustices at scale through community-informed red-teaming

**H&M implication**: The pipeline's six-agent architecture risks all four forms. Its source hierarchy may amplify testimonial injustice (privileging Western institutional voices). Its analytical frameworks may exhibit hermeneutical ignorance (lacking cultural context to interpret Global South experiences). Its English-language dominance may produce hermeneutical access injustice. The paper's recommendation to use AI for identifying testimonial injustices is particularly relevant: H&M could explicitly task one agent with detecting whose voices are missing from the analysis.

### 2.6 Data Sovereignty and Indigenous Knowledge in AI

Recent scholarship (2024-2026) has developed frameworks for integrating indigenous knowledge into AI systems:

- **Indigenous Data Sovereignty**: Recognizes "the rights of Indigenous Peoples to control and own their data" and challenges the treatment of indigenous data as "a neutral resource to be exploited." Key governance frameworks include the **CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance** (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics) and the **Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA)**. [PEER-REVIEWED: Rana, V. (2025). "Indigenous Data Sovereignty: A Catalyst for Ethical AI in Business." *Business & Society*.] [WEB-SOURCE: [OHCHR - Indigenous Sovereignty in the AI Era](https://www.ohchr.org/en/stories/2025/08/indigenous-sovereignty-ai-era)]

- **Cognitive Imperialism in AI**: Novel methodologies propose integrating indigenous knowledge systems into AI development to counter cognitive imperialism. The argument is that "Indigenous perspectives are not merely supplemental but integral to AI design, seeking to recalibrate the epistemic foundations upon which AI is built." [PEER-REVIEWED: "Cognitive imperialism in artificial intelligence." *AI & Society* (2024). Springer.]

- **Decolonial AI Sovereignty (DAIS) Model**: A conceptual framework grounded in Indigenous data sovereignty, relational ethics, and decolonial refusal. [PEER-REVIEWED: "Towards a decolonial AI sovereignty model for indigenous educational settings." *Cogent Education* (2026).]

**H&M implication**: The CARE Principles provide a concrete governance framework the pipeline could adapt: ensuring that analysis of claims affecting specific communities benefits those communities collectively, that communities have authority over how their knowledge is used, and that the pipeline bears responsibility for the effects of its analysis on those communities.

## 3. Participatory Research Methods for AI Pipeline Design

### 3.1 The Participatory Turn in AI Design

A systematic review of 95 participatory AI projects found that the majority remain in a **"consultative mode"** where preferences are elicited from stakeholders but analyzed by AI researchers, rather than genuine co-design. [PEER-REVIEWED: Delgado, F. et al. (2023). "The Participatory Turn in AI Design." *Proceedings of the ACM Conference on Computer-Supported Cooperative Work*. DOI: 10.1145/3617694.3623261]

**Critical finding on geographic skew**: North American and European projects comprise 52% of the sample, while Asia, Africa, Latin America, and Oceania collectively contribute fewer. Regional differences in theoretical approaches are notable: **participatory feminism** was identified solely in Latin American projects; **African projects emphasized decolonization and data justice** lenses; while Global North projects favor human-centered design or techno-solutionism. [PEER-REVIEWED: "Frameworks, Methods and Shared Tasks: Connecting Participatory AI to Trustworthy AI." *ACM FAccT 2025*. DOI: 10.1145/3715275.3732148]

Key insight: **"Participation is not the same thing as inclusion"** when it comes to technology development. Participation broadly gets others involved without specifying who, for how long, and to what end. [WEB-SOURCE: [Partnership on AI - Methods for Inclusion](https://partnershiponai.org/methodsforinclusion/)]

### 3.2 Four Levels of Participation (OECD Framework)

The OECD Participatory AI Framework defines four levels of increasing community involvement [WEB-SOURCE: [OECD.AI - Participatory AI Framework](https://oecd.ai/en/catalogue/tools/participatory-ai-framework)]:

1. **Consultation**: Input occurs outside the core AI development process (polling, deliberation). Stakeholders inform but don't shape.

2. **Contribution**: Time-limited involvement in specific pipeline stages (data collection, model validation, crowdsourcing). Stakeholders participate in bounded tasks.

3. **Collaboration**: Multiple engagement points throughout development; stakeholders shape model features and interrogate predictions.

4. **Co-design**: The most comprehensive form: all stakeholder groups discuss needs, values, and priorities across multiple pipeline stages, both with respect to the problem space and the technology.

**H&M current state**: The pipeline operates at Level 0 (no participation) or Level 1 at best (cites institutional reports that may have consulted communities). To achieve meaningful representation, it would need to reach Level 3 (Collaboration) or Level 4 (Co-design).

### 3.3 Design Justice: Costanza-Chock (2020)

Sasha Costanza-Chock (MIT) developed the **design justice** framework, drawing on Participatory Action Research and the Scandinavian co-design tradition. [PEER-REVIEWED: Costanza-Chock, S. (2020). *Design Justice: Community-Led Practices to Build the Worlds We Need*. MIT Press.]

**Core principles for H&M:**

- **"Nothing about us without us"**: "Those who are directly affected by the issues a project aims to address must be at the centre of the design process." At its best, a design justice process "is a form of community organising." [PEER-REVIEWED: Costanza-Chock 2020]

- **Designers as facilitators, not deciders**: Design justice methods position designers as facilitators who "amplify the work of communities by contributing their design skills," not as authorities who determine what matters. [PEER-REVIEWED: Costanza-Chock 2020]

- **Power redistribution**: Design justice "requires full inclusion of, accountability to, and ultimately control by people with direct lived experience of the conditions the design team is trying to change." [PEER-REVIEWED: Costanza-Chock 2020]

- **Intersectional analysis**: The framework includes intersectionality, "foregrounding gender, race, class and disability as interlocking systems" rather than treating them as separate variables. [PEER-REVIEWED: Costanza-Chock 2020]

### 3.4 Participatory Action Research (PAR) for AI Pipelines

PAR involves six building blocks applicable to AI pipeline design [PEER-REVIEWED: "Participatory action research." *Nature Reviews Methods Primers* (2023). DOI: 10.1038/s43586-023-00214-1]:

1. **Building relationships** with affected communities before designing systems
2. **Establishing working practices** that share power between developers and communities
3. **Establishing common understanding** of the problem as experienced by those affected
4. **Observing, gathering and generating materials** collaboratively
5. **Collaborative analysis** -- not just collaborative data collection
6. **Planning and taking action** together

Key insight: PAR "prioritizes the value of experiential knowledge for tackling problems caused by unequal and harmful social systems." It requires that designing the research methodology itself is "a collective endeavor where community members and researchers co-create a design."

**Known challenges**: Mismatches with institutional research infrastructure, risks of co-option, power inequalities within participatory processes, and the difficulty of decentralizing control. These can be addressed through "building PAR-friendly networks, cultivating critical communities, using critical reflexivity, and redistributing powers." [PEER-REVIEWED: Nature Reviews Methods Primers 2023]

### 3.5 Methods for Inclusion (Partnership on AI)

The Partnership on AI's "Methods for Inclusion" project identifies three critical gaps in standard participatory approaches [WEB-SOURCE: [Partnership on AI](https://partnershiponai.org/methodsforinclusion/)]:

1. **Identifying barriers to participation**: Exclusion exists due to lack of awareness, historical bias, and institutional policies.

2. **Expanding decision-making pathways**: Moving stakeholders beyond feedback roles to become "decision-makers directly influencing the creation and/or deployment of the technology."

3. **Valuing non-technical expertise**: Recognizing that "those who hold non-technical knowledge and experiences" contribute essential insights.

Specific recommended method: The **Diverse Voices methodology** (University of Washington Tech Policy Lab) coordinates experiential expert panels to review technology documentation, compensates non-technical stakeholders for their time and expertise, and generates insights about potential harms and unconsidered uses. [WEB-SOURCE: [Partnership on AI](https://partnershiponai.org/methodsforinclusion/)]

### 3.6 Community-Based System Dynamics (CBSD) for AI

Community-based system dynamics is a participatory method that uses **group modeling sessions** with diverse stakeholders to develop shared understanding of complex problems. It produces both informal maps and formal simulation models. [PEER-REVIEWED: "Participatory Machine Learning Using Community-Based System Dynamics." PMC7762892.]

**H&M implication**: CBSD offers a concrete method for the pipeline's "sense-making" stages. Instead of having agents generate causal models of why misinformation spreads, affected communities could participate in building those causal models, which would then inform the agents' analysis.

### 3.7 Practical Application: Report and Respond (Cameroon)

A practical example of participatory AI in the Global South: **Report and Respond**, an SMS-based system in Cameroon where communities report suspected misinformation via SMS, which is then checked and responded to through the same channel. This demonstrates participatory AI in low-connectivity, multilingual contexts. [WEB-SOURCE: [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/catalogue/tools/participatory-ai-framework)]

## 4. COVID Vaccine Access Inequality: Global South Local Research

### 4.1 African Researchers' Documentation

#### 4.1.1 Zbiri & Boukhalfa (Morocco, 2023)

**Authors**: Saad Zbiri (Mohammed VI University of Sciences and Health, Casablanca) and Chakib Boukhalfa (National School of Public Health, Rabat) -- both **Morocco-based researchers**. [PEER-REVIEWED: Zbiri, S. & Boukhalfa, C. (2023). "Inequality in COVID-19 vaccination in Africa." *International Journal for Equity in Health*. PMC10481897.]

**Key findings**: Analyzed 53 African nations (March 2021 to June 2022). By June 2022, Seychelles, Mauritius, Rwanda, and Morocco exceeded 60% vaccination, while Burundi, DRC, Madagascar, and Nigeria remained below 10%. Two factors independently predicted vaccination rates: COVID-19 severity (total cases per million) and Human Development Index.

**Local perspective**: The inequality was not merely about supply but about structural capacity to absorb and distribute vaccines, reflecting deeper development inequities.

#### 4.1.2 Kunyenje, Chirwa & Mfutso-Bengo (Malawi, 2023)

**Authors**: Chifundo Annessia Kunyenje, Gowokani Chijere Chirwa, Joseph Mfutso-Bengo -- primarily based at **Kamuzu University of Health Sciences, Malawi**, with affiliations at the University of Malawi and Malawi's Ministry of Health. [PEER-REVIEWED: Kunyenje et al. (2023). "COVID-19 vaccine inequity in African low-income countries." *Frontiers in Public Health*. DOI: 10.3389/fpubh.2023.1087662.]

**Key findings from local perspective**:
- Among the bottom 10 lowest-income nations, vaccination coverage ranged from under 1% (Burundi) to 55.96% (Mozambique), versus 67-105% in wealthy countries
- By December 2022, Africa had only 28% population fully vaccinated
- In Malawi specifically, widespread beliefs that vaccines "contain a microchip or DNA marker" undermined uptake
- Nations faced "ethical dilemmas" between vaccine procurement and maintaining broader health services
- Weak infrastructure including inadequate cold-storage facilities and vast distances hindered rural distribution
- Low-income nations commenced purchases only in January 2021, leaving them "relatively out of stock"

**Critical local documentation**: The Malawian researchers documented how donated vaccines often arrived with short notice and short shelf lives, making campaign planning nearly impossible and keeping coverage rates low despite theoretical supply.

#### 4.1.3 Wei, Kamande & Lang'at (Kenya/Taiwan, 2023)

**Authors**: Calvin R Wei (Taiwan), Samuel Kamande (Maseno University, Kenya), Godwin C Lang'at (University of Nairobi, Kenya -- corresponding author). Two of three authors are **Kenya-based**. [PEER-REVIEWED: Wei, Kamande & Lang'at (2023). "Vaccine inequity: a threat to Africa's recovery from COVID-19." *Tropical Medicine and Health*. PMC10729430.]

**Key findings from local perspective**:
- High-income countries bypassed COVAX to secure bilateral manufacturer agreements
- South Africa paid $5+ per dose versus the European Commission's $3.50, despite lower GDP
- Operational costs consumed approximately 60% of campaign budgets in African nations
- "Misinformation regarding COVID-19 vaccine, lack of trust in politicians and pharmaceutical industry" were significant drivers of hesitancy
- Solution emphasis on **local vaccine manufacturing** to reduce foreign dependency

#### 4.1.4 Vaccine Hesitancy in the Colonial Context

A systematic review documented that vaccine hesitancy in Sub-Saharan Africa "occurs against a backdrop of colonialism and inequities in global health research, socio-cultural complexities, poor government response in debunking social and traditional media theories and poor community involvement in public health measures." [PEER-REVIEWED: "Vaccine Hesitancy in Sub-Saharan Africa in the Context of COVID-19 Vaccination Exercise." PMC9944066.]

**H&M implication**: This is critical context the pipeline currently lacks. When the pipeline encounters vaccine-skeptical narratives from African contexts, it needs to understand that this skepticism has historically grounded roots in colonial-era medical experimentation, contemporary pharmaceutical exploitation, and legitimate structural grievances, not just "misinformation."

### 4.2 South Asian Researchers' Documentation (India)

#### 4.2.1 The CoWIN Digital Divide

India's vaccine rollout centered on the **CoWIN digital platform**, which created massive access barriers:

- CoWIN was **initially available only in English**, spoken by approximately 10% of India's population (12% urban, 3% rural). India has 22 official languages. [WEB-SOURCE: [LSE South Asia - India Digital Divide](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/southasia/2021/06/28/india-digital-divide-and-the-promise-of-vaccination-for-all/)]

- India's Supreme Court (June 2, 2021) warned that "a vaccination policy relying exclusively on a digital portal would fail to achieve universal immunisation," citing 2018 data showing **merely 4% of rural households and 23% of urban households possessed a computer**. [WEB-SOURCE: [LSE South Asia](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/southasia/2021/06/28/india-digital-divide-and-the-promise-of-vaccination-for-all/)]

- Internet density was 25% in rural areas versus 98% in urban areas. 55,000 villages lacked adequate mobile network coverage. [WEB-SOURCE: [LSE South Asia](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/southasia/2021/06/28/india-digital-divide-and-the-promise-of-vaccination-for-all/)]

- Vaccines administered in urban districts were **1.7 times more** than in rural districts. [WEB-SOURCE: [CSIS - Balancing Digital India and Vaccine Equity](https://www.csis.org/blogs/new-perspectives-asia/balancing-digital-india-and-vaccine-equity)]

- A Swabhiman survey found "86% of differently-abled people did not know how to use the technology while 61% said they would not be able to afford to buy a new smartphone." [WEB-SOURCE: [LSE South Asia](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/southasia/2021/06/28/india-digital-divide-and-the-promise-of-vaccination-for-all/)]

- Privileged urban populations used CoWIN to book appointments at rural vaccination centers, **depleting local resources** intended for rural populations. [WEB-SOURCE: [LSE South Asia](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/southasia/2021/06/28/india-digital-divide-and-the-promise-of-vaccination-for-all/)]

- Assam's South Salmara district had India's lowest vaccination rate at 1.96%, while Kamrup Metro in the same state ranked in the top seven nationally. [WEB-SOURCE: [Down to Earth - Assam vaccination digital divide](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/health/how-covid-19-vaccination-figures-from-assam-highlight-india-s-digital-divide-77079)]

**Local documentation source**: Rohit Sharma, West Bengal National University of Juridical Sciences, Kolkata. [WEB-SOURCE: [LSE South Asia](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/southasia/2021/06/28/india-digital-divide-and-the-promise-of-vaccination-for-all/)]

#### 4.2.2 Gender Divide

Research found that as males' access to the internet increases, vaccination coverage also increases, attributed to India's reliance on CoWIN and the associated digital divide where **males have greater digital access than females**. In Assam, 75.4% of urban women had mobile phones versus 53.9% of rural women. [WEB-SOURCE: [Down to Earth](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/health/how-covid-19-vaccination-figures-from-assam-highlight-india-s-digital-divide-77079)]

### 4.3 Latin American Researchers' Documentation

#### 4.3.1 PAHO Systematic Documentation (2022)

**Authors**: Jimbo-Sotomayor et al. documented the introduction of COVID-19 vaccines in Argentina, Belize, Brazil, Costa Rica, Panama, and Peru (December 2021 to September 2022). [PEER-REVIEWED: Jimbo-Sotomayor et al. "Systematic documentation of the introduction of COVID-19 vaccines in Latin America and the Caribbean." *Pan American Journal of Public Health*.]

**Key findings from local perspective**:
- **Strengths**: Health workers' commitment, evidence-based decision-making, participation of national immunization technical advisory groups
- **Barriers**: Anti-vaccination group actions, electronic registry malfunctions, vaccine shortages, distribution delays, and critically "the scarcity of health personnel at the local level"
- Two years into the pandemic, only **46.1%** of the general population in lower-middle income countries in the Americas was fully vaccinated (July 2022)

#### 4.3.2 Local Manufacturing Response

PAHO selected **Bio-Manguinhos Institute at Fiocruz (Brazil)** and **Sinergium Biotech (Argentina)** as regional hubs for mRNA vaccine development and production, representing a local manufacturing response to global supply inequity. [WEB-SOURCE: [PAHO - Vaccine equity producers](https://www.paho.org/en/stories/vaccine-equity-producers-work-together-advance-mrna-technology-latin-america)]

### 4.4 Synthesis: What Global South Vaccine Research Reveals

The local researcher perspective differs from the Oxfam/MSF framing in several important ways:

| Dimension | Western Institutional Framing (Oxfam/MSF) | Local Researcher Framing |
|-----------|-------------------------------------------|-------------------------|
| **Primary problem** | Supply inequality, IP barriers | Structural capacity gaps + supply inequality |
| **Agency** | Global South as recipient/victim | Global South as researcher/manufacturer/innovator |
| **Hesitancy** | Misinformation to be corrected | Historically grounded distrust requiring structural solutions |
| **Solution** | Donate more vaccines, waive IP | Build local manufacturing, strengthen health systems |
| **Digital access** | Rarely discussed | Central barrier (CoWIN, digital registries) |
| **Gender** | General equity concern | Specific digital-gender intersection documented |
| **Historical context** | Mentioned abstractly | Colonial medical experimentation, pharmaceutical exploitation |

**H&M implication**: When the pipeline analyzes vaccine equity claims, it needs to represent the local researcher perspective, not just the Western institutional perspective. The difference is not minor: it shifts the narrative from "victims who need help" to "researchers and innovators constrained by structural inequities they are actively working to overcome."

## 5. Structural Recommendations for the H&M Pipeline

Based on the research across all four focus questions, the following structural changes would address the Global South representation gap:

### 5.1 Source Architecture Changes

**R1: Pluralistic Evidence Hierarchy**
Replace the implicit hierarchy (Western peer-reviewed > institutional reports > community testimony) with an explicit "ecology of knowledges" (Santos) model where:
- Community-based fact-checking organizations (Africa Check, BOOM, Chequeado) are treated as primary sources for context and verification
- Local researchers' publications are actively searched and cited alongside Western institutional reports
- Experiential testimony is treated as valid evidence for the lived-experience dimension of any claim, clearly distinguished from but not subordinated to statistical evidence

**R2: Regional Source Registries**
Create explicit source registries for major Global South regions, including:
- African: Africa Check, Africa Facts Network members, Africa CDC, African Journal of Science and Technology, local university research
- South Asian: BOOM, Alt News, Indian research institutions, local language media
- Latin American: LatamChequea network (48 organizations), PAHO, Fiocruz, local university research
- These registries should be consulted by default, not only when a claim is explicitly about those regions

**R3: Language-Aware Source Search**
The pipeline's evidence-gathering agents should include prompts to search for sources in relevant non-English languages and explicitly flag when analysis relies solely on English-language sources for claims affecting non-English-speaking populations.

### 5.2 Analytical Framework Changes

**R4: Epistemic Provenance Tracking**
Each pipeline output should explicitly track and display the epistemic provenance of its evidence: which knowledge traditions, geographic regions, and community types are represented. This makes the "abyssal lines" (Santos) visible rather than invisible.

**R5: Counter-Hegemonic Analysis Agent**
Add or repurpose an existing agent to perform a specific "epistemic audit" function: whose voices are present in this analysis? Whose are absent? What knowledge traditions frame the analytical categories? This agent would draw on Ndlovu-Gatsheni's framework to identify when analysis is inadvertently reproducing the "intellectual division of labor."

**R6: Structural Context for Skepticism**
When the pipeline encounters health skepticism from Global South communities, it should automatically surface the historical context (colonial medical exploitation, contemporary pharmaceutical inequities) that makes that skepticism at least partially structurally grounded, rather than treating it purely as "misinformation."

### 5.3 Participatory Design Changes

**R7: Community Advisory Input Mechanism**
Inspired by Maldita.es's "superpowers" model and BOOM's WhatsApp tipline, the pipeline could implement a structured mechanism for domain experts from affected communities to provide context that informs analysis. This moves the pipeline from Level 0 to Level 2 (Contribution) on the OECD participation scale.

**R8: Collaborative Causal Modeling**
Drawing on Community-Based System Dynamics (CBSD), the pipeline's sense-making stages could incorporate community-built causal models of why specific narratives circulate in specific communities, rather than relying solely on agent-generated models.

**R9: CARE Principles Adaptation**
Adapt the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance to the pipeline's use of community knowledge:
- **Collective benefit**: Analysis of claims affecting specific communities should benefit those communities
- **Authority to control**: Communities should have input into how their knowledge and experiences are represented
- **Responsibility**: The pipeline bears responsibility for effects of its analysis on affected communities
- **Ethics**: The pipeline should not extract community knowledge without accountability

### 5.4 What This Does NOT Mean

To maintain analytical rigor while improving representation:
- It does not mean treating all knowledge claims as equally valid regardless of evidence
- It does not mean privileging community testimony over scientific evidence for empirical claims
- It does mean recognizing that different types of knowledge are valid for different dimensions of analysis
- It does mean making the epistemic choices visible rather than invisible
- It does mean actively seeking out Global South researchers' own analyses rather than relying solely on Western institutional framing of Global South experiences
