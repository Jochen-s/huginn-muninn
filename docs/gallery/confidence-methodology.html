How Confidence Scores Work

Methodology

What the Confidence Score Means

The overall confidence score (0.0 to 1.0, shown as a percentage) reflects how well-sourced the claim's origins are. It is not a truth score. A high score means the claim draws on verifiable, well-documented sources. A low score means the claim relies on unverifiable or low-quality sources.

How the Score is Calculated

Three factors determine the confidence score:

  1. Source quality base: The average quality of the sources the Origin Tracer identified. Peer-reviewed journals and government data score high. Anonymous social media posts score low.
  2. Complexity adjustment: Multi-actor claims with many moving parts get dampened because complexity breeds uncertainty. A simple factual claim starts from a higher base than a claim involving dozens of actors and nested causal chains.
  3. Auditor adjustment: The Adversarial Auditor reviews the entire pipeline's work and adjusts the score up or down based on quality, completeness, and bias checks.

The Five Dimensions (Evidence Certainty Framework)

Each claim is also assessed across five dimensions. These are weighted to produce a composite score:

DimensionWeightWhat It Measures
Evidence Quality30%How strong is the underlying evidence? Are there controlled studies, systematic reviews, or only anecdotes?
Source Reliability20%How credible are the sources? Do they have editorial standards, correction policies, or peer review?
Claim Testability15%Can the claim be checked against observable data? Or is it unfalsifiable by design?
Expert Consensus20%Do domain experts agree on this topic? Is there genuine scientific debate, or manufactured controversy?
Internal Coherence15%Is the claim logically consistent? Do its sub-claims support each other, or contradict?

ECF Levels

LevelComposite ScoreWhat It Means

LOW0.25 to 0.49Weak evidence, limited credible sources, claims difficult to verify
VERY LOWBelow 0.25No credible evidence identified, untestable claims, no expert support

Source Tier System

Every source identified by the Origin Tracer is classified into one of four tiers:

TierLabelExamples
1Primary/InstitutionalPeer-reviewed journals (Nature, The Lancet), government health agencies (WHO, CDC), official statistics
2Quality JournalismMajor news organizations with editorial standards and corrections policies (Reuters, AP News, BBC)
3Secondary/CommentaryEstablished blogs, opinion outlets, think tanks with disclosed funding
4Unverified/SocialSocial media posts, anonymous blogs, forums, unattributed content

The confidence score starts from the average tier score of all identified sources. Tier 1 sources contribute 0.9, Tier 2 contributes 0.7, Tier 3 contributes 0.5, and Tier 4 contributes 0.3.

What the Score is NOT

  • Not a truth score. A high confidence score does not mean the claim is true. It means the claim is well-sourced. Well-sourced claims can still be wrong. Poorly-sourced claims can still be correct.
  • Not a censorship signal. This score must never be used as an automated content-moderation trigger, takedown signal, or input to automated decisions affecting content visibility.
  • Not a legal determination. This analysis is informational. It does not constitute legal advice or regulatory compliance.
  • Not a replacement for human judgment. The score is advisory. It provides structured information to help humans form their own assessments.

This framework complies with the advisory-only principle: all scoring fields carry explicit warnings that they must not be used for automated content moderation without human review (per GDPR Art. 22, EU AI Act Annex III, UK OSA s.179).

Counter-Narrative Quality Score Beta

Some analyses include a Counter-Narrative Quality Score (CNQS). This is an experimental framework that evaluates how well institutions (governments, health agencies, media organizations) have responded to a claim. It does not evaluate whether the claim is true.

The CNQS has eight dimensions, each scored 1 to 5:

  1. Respect for Audience: Does the institutional response treat the audience as capable adults, or talk down to them?
  2. Acknowledgment of Uncertainty: Does the response honestly acknowledge what is not yet known?
  3. Proportionality of Response: Is the response proportionate to the actual risk, or overblown/dismissive?
  4. Institutional Interest Transparency: Does the response disclose the institution's own interests and potential conflicts?
  5. Alternative Explanation Quality: Does the response offer substantive alternative explanations, or just dismiss the claim?
  6. Factual Accuracy: Are the facts cited in the response accurate and verifiable?
  7. Tone Calibration: Is the tone appropriate for the audience and context?
  8. Cognitive Load Management: Is the response clear and digestible, or overwhelming?

A score of 1 on any dimension triggers a "critical failure" flag, indicating a fundamental problem with the institutional response.

The CNQS is marked Beta because the scoring framework is new and has not yet been empirically validated against real-world outcomes. Treat it as a structured conversation starter, not a definitive assessment.
